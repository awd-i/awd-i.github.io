Introduction

This past quarter, I’ve been taking a class called CS 312, where we learn deep learning through running empirical experiments on different properties of ML systems.

On one of my more recent course assignments, one technique that really caught my eye was Copy-Paste Language Models (CPLMs) (Godey & Artzi, 2026a)1. I thought the method was pretty intuitive, and the architecture behind it was very interesting.

In our class experiments, we found that on d8 models (8 blocks, width 512, about 35M parameters), that CPLM surprisingly effective at making models more expressive.

The reason this occurs is because it bypasses the rank limit of the output head. Past literature states that a standard softmax head with hidden size d can only produce next-token log-probabilities of rank at most d. This is called the softmax bottleneck of Yang et al. (2018)2, which Godey et al. (2024)14 link to performance saturation in small LMs. With a rank r head, our limit is r, however as we come to find out, the copy path is not subject to that constraint.

How does CPLM work?

CPLM belongs to the family of pointer and copy mechanisms (Vinyals et al., 2015; Gu et al., 2016; See et al., 2017; Merity et al., 2017)3–6. At an abstract level, the idea of these mechanisms is to give the model a second route to the output. CPLM adds a “copy pointer”, which is directed to a position in the context. The model can then paste a token from that location, on top of scoring every vocabulary item through the output head, which ends up giving us a more expressive probability distribution.

Overview of Class Experiments (d8)

Say we have a base LM with hidden state ht at position t:

Output head
pgen(y | x≤t) = softmax(Wo ht)y

We then build a copy pointer: the current hidden state (our context) becomes a “query” and the hidden states at past positions become “keys”, along with one extra learned “sink” key. We then match and softmax them together to get a “copy probability” for each position. In essence, we are essentially running a single attention head over earlier positions:

Score for position i
st,i = (Wq ht)⊤ (Wk hi) / √d
Score for the sink
st,∅ = (Wq ht)⊤ k∅ / √d
Copy weight
at,i = exp(st,i)exp(st,∅) + Σj≤t exp(st,j)
Sink weight
at,∅ = exp(st,∅)exp(st,∅) + Σj≤t exp(st,j)

Pointing at position i means “paste the token at i”, so the copy distribution over the vocabulary sums the pointer’s weight over every occurrence of a token:

Copy distribution
mt(y) = Σi≤t : xi=y at,i

The sink is the pointer’s way of saying “nothing here is worth copying”: whatever weight it puts on the sink (at,∅) is handed back to the normal output head.

A gate then decides how much to trust the pointer. Instead of a separate module, the gate is one extra <copy> option added to the output softmax, and its probability is the gate:

Gate
ct = softmaxvocabulary ∪ {<copy>}(output logits)<copy>

Using the gate and the sink, we create a final next-token distribution using the mixture:

Mixture
p(y) = (1 − ct + ct · at,∅) · pgen(y) + ct · mt(y)

Equivalently, the copy head gets an effective weight of λt = ct(1 − at,∅) and the output head gets the rest. This is the gate/sink design from Godey & Artzi (2026a)1.

Why does it work?

CPLM is effective because a softmax head of rank r can only produce log-probability matrices of rank at most r (Yang et al., 2018)2. Using the copy term, the probability can be placed directly onto context tokens without going through Wo, so we're not limited to a rank-r head's expressiveness. This also predicts that the copy head should help more as the head gets narrower.

My rank sweep tests this directly. CPLM beat plain softmax at every head rank, by 0.021 nats at full rank and by up to 0.122 nats at rank 32 on my d8 model, roughly a sixfold larger gain at the narrowest head.

We can also see that the LM head is also a bottleneck for gradients. Since the training signal reaching the backbone is compressed through the head (Godey & Artzi, 2026b)9. This means that the copy head also gives a gives the backbone a second gradient path that avoids it.

Is Adding the Head Post-Training Beneficial?

After seeing these results in pre-training, as well as reading the apper, I got curious about what would happen if we added the head after training. My intuition was that there could be a benefit to doing so, because:

  1. Again, the bottleneck should still persist. The model's normal output head is a bottleneck (Yang et al., 2018)2, and finishing training does not remove it.
  2. Hypothetically, the information should be there, since finished models already track when something is about to be repeated: ordinary attention looks back at earlier occurrences, the behaviour attributed to induction heads (Olsson et al., 2022)7. A late head could give that signal a direct route to the output, since "the next token is this one again" is plausibly already sitting in the model's internal state.
  3. Repeats are common in text, in my d8 validation data, 49% of positions were tokens already in the context.
  4. Similar mechanisms have worked before. Chang et al. (2023)8 added pointer-style heads to pretrained GPT-2, T5, and BART models, continued training, and reported gains.
  5. The head seems to learn fast, at least in d8 models, the from-scratch advantage was fully formed within the first 15% of training in my assignment experiemtns, so intuitively the head should not need a whole pre-training run to become useful.

However, it should be mentioned that there is a counterargument, from the CPLM paper itself. Godey & Artzi (2026a, Section 4.1)1 attribute most of the benefit to the gradients the copy head sends into the backbone (i.e. to the layers reorganizing around the head during training). When the backbone was shielded from that signal1, only about 25% of the gain remained (0.008 of 0.031 nats). Pre-trained models never had the chance to reorganize, and the authors argue in Section 5 that a head added after pretraining "cannot fully undo" the effect of the standard head's bottleneck on the backbone, building on their earlier gradient-bottleneck analysis (Godey & Artzi, 2026b)9.

From both of these, I hypothesized a middle position:

A copy head added to a finished model hypothetically could recover part of the gain a from-scratch head gets. How much would depend on whether the model is allowed to adapt to it.

Nontrivially, I also suspected there could be larger gains if the model is allowed to keep adapting.

Three ways to add a head to a finished model

The hypothesis says the gain should depend on how much the model is allowed to adapt. So I add the same head in three ways, from least to most adaptation:

VersionWhat trainsWhat is being tested
FrozenOnly the copy headIs the information already in the finished model?
ShieldedEverything, but the pointer reads a detached copy of the hidden state, so its queries and keys cannot reshape the layersDoes adaptation matter if the pointer cannot steer it? This is the late-head version of the paper's stop-gradient ablation1
Whole modelEverything, with gradients from the head flowing into the layersThe most a late head can get

In order to make sure that we're getting fair comparisons, I used:

  • A control run using the same model trained for the same extra tokens with no head. Extra training on fresh web text lowers the loss by itself. Thus every whole-model and shielded gain below is measured against this control, and not against the original checkpoint.
  • Parameter-matched adapters: since adding a copy-head's gain could just be the result of "more-parameters", we create a small two-layer residual adapter on the final hidden state (Houlsby et al., 2019)29 with the same number of parameters as the copy head.

For the head itself, we use a published gate-and-sink design. We initialize it with the gate almost closed (about 0.2% on average), so at the moment of insertion the model's predictions are unchanged: 2.6671 nats with the head on against 2.6673 with it off.

For training: We use 350M tokens of FineWeb-Edu12 from eight crawls dated late 2024 to mid 2025, in sequences of up to 2,048 tokens. (I'd like to note that this is under 2% of the 20B tokens the authors used to train these models). Learning rates were chosen by short sweeps (0.003 for the frozen head, and a model rate of 0.001 with a head rate of 0.01 for the whole-model and shielded runs).

Our reference is what a from-scratch head is worth, which we calculate to be the loss of the authors' vanilla model minus the loss of their copy-head twin, on the same five sets. "Share recovered" is a late head's gain divided by that. As a calibration check, my FinePDFs perplexities for the vanilla models are within 3 to 4% of the ones the paper reports.

For the whole-model, shielded and control runs have three seeds each.

A from-scratch head is worth most on small models and repetitive text

Two things in this figure helped shape the rest of the project. On the web text, the head's value shrunk as the model grew (from 0.049 nats at 135M to 0.033 at 360M to nothing at 1.7B for the full-attention models). On long PDFs, which are much more repetitive, it stayed large at every size (0.16 to 0.31 nats). Thus, empirically, a small model is where a late head has the most to recover. The 135M sliding-window pair, where the from-scratch gain is largest (0.057 nats on web text, 0.353 on PDFs), is the one I train on.

From the results, I saw that the hypothesis held on this model, in both of its parts. To begin with, a late head recovered part of the gain: even with the model completely frozen, the head gets about a quarter of what a from-scratch head gets. That is the same fraction the paper found when it blocked the head's gradients during pretraining (25%)1, which suggests that the copy signal is already present in a finished model and that a quarter of the benefit is available without touching it.

The late head also recovered more once the model could adapt. Letting the layers train with the pointer shielded roughly doubles the frozen figure, to 46 to 51%, and letting the head's gradients reach the layers adds more again, to 58 to 62%. Even so, late heads did not recover the full difference. After 350M tokens the whole-model run was still about 40% short of the from-scratch head, which fits the authors' argument that a late head cannot fully undo what the standard head did to the model during pretraining.

Figure 3 shows that the head learns quickly: more than two thirds of each run's final gain is there after the first 50M tokens. The curves flatten towards 350M, but the learning rate is also decaying to zero by then, so this run cannot tell me whether longer training would close more of the gap.

Where the gain comes from. It comes entirely from repeated tokens. In the whole-model run, loss on repeated tokens falls by 0.076 nats on web text and 0.335 on PDFs, while loss on new tokens rises by 0.022 and 0.055. The shielded and frozen runs show the same pattern at smaller size.

How stable it is. The three seeds agree to within 0.0001 nats on web text and 0.002 on PDFs. That is tighter than real run-to-run variation would be, because the seeds share the same training documents and starting checkpoint.

On other model families, the gain depends on the model

The released checkpoints all come from one training recipe, so I tried the frozen head on four models from other groups: GPT-218, Pythia-160M19, Qwen2.5-0.5B20 and Gemma 3 270M30.

The published head trained on GPT-2 (0.016 nats) but stayed at exactly zero on Pythia, Qwen and Gemma, which was due to scale. The final hidden state of the authors' 135M model had a typical magnitude near 0.3, while the others sat between 4 and 12 - which saturated the pointer at the start so that it never moved. I did two changes to help fix this issue: rescaling the hidden state before the pointer reads it, and adding a gate that does not depend on the size of the model's logits. For each model I swept between six and fourteen head settings and three or four adapter learning rates, then ran the best of each for the full 350M tokens on GPT-2, Pythia and Qwen.

From the results, I saw how much the gain is depends on the model. On GPT-2 the frozen head gained 0.043 nats on web text, against 0.012 for the frozen head on the authors' own 135M model. Pythia gains 0.017. On Qwen, the head gained less than 0.01 nats in every setting I tried. Comparisons across models are rough, since each has its own tokenizer and a nat per token is not the same unit in each.

The adapter wins on web text and loses everywhere else. On web text from the crawls it trained on, the adapter beats the head on all three models, by a factor of three on GPT-2. On the older crawl its lead shrinks, to nothing on Qwen. On long PDFs it made every model worse, by 0.05 to 0.10 nats, while the head gained about as much on PDFs as on web text. So the adapter mostly learns what this web text looks like and the head learns something that carries to other text, however this doesn't necessarily mean that the head is the better use of 0.5% of parameters on text like the training data.

The cost to new tokens, also shows up. With the head on, tokens that are not already in the context get 0.011 to 0.039 nats worse on web text, as they did on the 135M model.

One possible explanation for Qwen is that such models already copy well through ordinary attention, leaving little for a pointer to add. I did not test this directly.

The gain is largest where copying is the task

For most of my analysis, I've only measured loss averaged over ordinary text. Which in practice says little about the cases a copy head exists for, where the right output is sitting in the context and the model has to find it. So I scored the same checkpoints on three kinds of test, each against its own control.

The first is answer scoring on SQuAD42: a passage, a question, then the reference answer, scored as loss per answer token on all 10,570 validation questions. Alongside it I used a repeated list of 8 names with random access codes, written twice, where the codes in the second copy are scored. Finally, to see whether any of this reaches standard benchmarks, I ran two zero-shot tasks, SciQ25 and LAMBADA43, through lm-evaluation-harness44.

The gain is far larger where copying is the task. On the 135M model a late head lowers loss on SQuAD answers by 0.26 to 0.29 nats per token, against 0.036 on web text. That is 70 to 80% of what the from-scratch head gets (0.36).

It carries to the other model groups, and the adapter's gain does not. The frozen head gains 0.33 on GPT-2, 0.17 on Pythia and 0.07 on Qwen, roughly ten times each model's gain on web text. The same-size adapter makes GPT-2 and Pythia slightly worse on SQuAD and gains 0.01 on Qwen. This is the clearest evidence I have that the head's gain is about copying and not about extra parameters.

The top guess rarely changes. The late head raises the probability of the right token much more often than it changes which token ranks first. With the whole-model head, the share of SQuAD answer tokens ranked first goes from 66.5% to 67.2%. Most of the loss drop is on the first answer token, about 1 nat on every 135M model, which is the point where the model has to locate the answer.

Nothing is gained on the repeated list: every model already predicted 96 to 100% of the second copy's code tokens through ordinary attention, and late heads move the loss by less than 0.01. I had expected this to be the test where the head gained the most.

On images, only editing has tokens to copy, and the editing model already copies them

If a copy head helps text transformers, I expected it to help discrete-token image models too. Models such as LlamaGen31, Emu332, Chameleon37 and Janus-Pro38 predict the next image token from a fixed codebook, the same way a language model predicts the next word, so the head fits them without changes.

Since every gain so far came from tokens that already appear in the context, the first thing to check costs almost nothing and involves no model: how often do image tokens repeat? I tokenised images with LlamaGen's tokenizer (16,384 codes, 256 tokens per image) and counted.

The counts in Figure 9 split sharply by setting. When generating a single image, only 2% of next tokens could be copied, because a 256-token image uses about 250 different codes and so there is almost nothing to copy. I had predicted 10 to 30%, and I was wrong. Video does only a little better, at 17% for the next frame, and the reason is that the tokenizer is noisy: shifting an image by a single pixel changes more than a third of its codes, so two nearly identical frames mostly get different tokens.

Editing is the exception. Most of an edited image is identical to its source, and so are its tokens, which puts the share at 61%, in the same range as text. So the hypothesis fails for plain image generation before any model is involved, and survives in a narrower form: a copy head could help an image model that edits.

Where both images are generated, the "unchanged" parts are regenerated and almost nothing matches. No large dataset comes close to MagicBrush, and only one slice of OmniEdit clears the 30% mark I set in advance.

The tokenizer matters as much as the data. Emu3's tokenizer uses 4,096 tokens per image and is far more sensitive than LlamaGen's: shifting an image by one pixel leaves only 5% of its codes unchanged, and only 3% of a video frame's tokens match the previous frame. On MagicBrush edit pairs, 37% of tokens match the source, against 61% with LlamaGen's tokenizer. So editing is still the one setting that clears the bar, but by a smaller margin on the larger model's tokenizer.

The editing experiment. A count of matching tokens is an opportunity, not a gain. An ordinary attention layer can also copy the token at the same position, and if the editing model already does that well, a pointer has nothing to add. So the test has two steps, cheapest first.

I do not need to train an editing model to run it. EditAR45 is a public 775M-parameter editing model built on LlamaGen. It reads the source image's 1,024 tokens, then the instruction, then writes the edited image's 1,024 tokens, so the tokens worth copying are already in its context.

  1. With no training, count how many edited-image tokens equal the source token at the same position under EditAR's own tokenizer, and measure how much loss EditAR still has on exactly those tokens. That product is the most a perfect pointer could gain. If it is small, I stop here.
  2. Frozen head against a same-size adapter, trained on PIPE46, an 888,000-pair set that EditAR was itself trained on, so the adapter cannot win just by adapting to new data. The head is tried both unchanged from text and with a pointer that is told the grid position, since on images the token worth copying is the one at the same position.

The pass mark is missed on every set, so I did not go on to step 2. The token counts held up: 59 to 71% of edited tokens match the source on PIPE and MagicBrush, as I predicted. What I got wrong was the model. I expected 1 to 3 nats of loss left on those tokens, and it is 0.2 to 0.3, because EditAR already ranks the source token first 99% of the time.

Its loss is somewhere a pointer cannot reach. On PIPE the tokens that differ from the source are 29% of the tokens and 93% of the loss, at about 6 nats each. And on those tokens the model already copies too much: where the edit changed a token, EditAR still gives the old source token 45% of the probability. A copy head would push further in that direction.

So the image hypothesis fails in both forms I could test. Plain generation has nothing to copy, and an editing model that can see its source copies well through ordinary attention. That fits the explanation I floated for Qwen and Gemma, that a pointer adds little to a model that already copies, though here it is measured directly. The caveats are that this is one model, trained with the source in its context, and an upper bound from scored tokens. I did not train a head on it or generate edits.

Limitations

There are several things I cannot claim yet, and the largest is how far the result carries beyond the model family it was designed for. With the model frozen, the head gains 0.043 nats on GPT-2 and 0.017 on Pythia, and under 0.01 on Qwen and Gemma. Whether letting the model adapt rescues it on those two is untested, and Gemma has only a short sweep. The whole-model gains on GPT-2 and Pythia are provisional as well: they are sweep runs, scored on the documents used to pick the rates, at 100M tokens against 350M for the frozen runs, so the held-back figures need the full-length runs. On Pythia, moreover, the control and the run with the head sit at different model rates, because each is at its own best, and since best is chosen on web text, choosing on long PDFs would pick much lower model rates on both models. Each of these is one seed.

Size is a second limit. Everything trained here is 135M to 0.5B, and on web text the from-scratch head is worth nothing at 1.7B, so I would not expect a late head to help there either.

The task results need care too. Where copying is the task the head's gain is large, 0.26 to 0.29 nats on SQuAD answers and about 4 points of SciQ accuracy, but it raises the probability of the right token far more often than it changes the model's top guess. LAMBADA remains unexplained: frozen heads gain 0.7 to 2.7 points of accuracy, while the whole-model and shielded heads gain none. Nor does the head beat an adapter on every copying test. I also scored lookups of random access codes in lists of 4 to 48 records, and on Pythia and Qwen the same-size adapter gained about as much as the head or more, so the head's edge over an adapter holds on SQuAD and not on that test. Those lookups also suggest that a late head does not learn what a trained-in head learns: on the 48-record list, which is longer than the sliding-window model's attention window, the from-scratch head cut the loss by 1.83 nats and late heads by 0.28 to 0.44.

Finally, the shares themselves are approximate. The from-scratch reference is computed on the full evaluation sets and the late-head gains on the held-back part of them, and the whole-model learning rates sit at the edge of the grid I swept.

Conclusion

On the model family the technique was designed for, the answer is yes on both counts. A copy head added after training recovers about a quarter of the from-scratch gain with the model frozen and about 60% when the whole model trains, on held-back text, across three seeds.
